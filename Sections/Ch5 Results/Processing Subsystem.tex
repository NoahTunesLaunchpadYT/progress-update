\section{Windowing}
The Hann (with $\alpha = 0.5$) and Hamming windows are first evaluated for the prominence of their main lobe and sidelobes
in the frequency domain on the synthetic datasets. Figure \ref{fig:clean filters} shows the
performance of each filter on a noise-free sine wave, and Figure \ref{fig:noisy filters}
shows the same comparison at an SNR of 1.

\begin{figure}
    \incfig[0.7]{filter_clear_comparison}
    \caption[Window filter comparison on a noise-free signal.]{FFT of a single-frequency
    noise-free signal with no window, a Hann window, and a Hamming window. The x-axis is
    frequency bin and the y-axis is power in dB.}
    \label{fig:clean filters}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}
    \incfig[0.7]{filter_noisy_comparison}
    \caption[Window filter comparison at SNR of 1.]{FFT of a single-frequency signal at
    SNR~$= 1$ with no window, a Hann window, and a Hamming window. The x-axis is frequency
    bin and the y-axis is power in dB.}
    \label{fig:noisy filters}
\end{figure}

In the noise-free case all three options correctly produce a peak near bin 334. The Hann
window achieves the greatest global sidelobe suppression, with a 100\,dB reduction in power
more than 40 bins from the target. The Hamming window achieves only 70\,dB global
suppression, but features a steeper drop-off near the target bin, which may improve
resolution of closely spaced targets. Under an SNR of 1, unwindowed spectral leakage
deforms the peak at bin 334. The Hann and Hamming windows perform similarly at a global
level, but the Hamming window maintains a slightly higher peak and less leakage into
adjacent bins.

In terms of computational performance, the latency contribution of the filters does not
significantly differ across implementations. Figure \ref{fig:latency} shows the time
taken to process a single frame of 10\,000 chirps each with 767 samples, averaged over
50 runs.

\begin{figure}
    \incfig[0.7]{filter_latency_comparison}
    \caption[Windowing stage latency across implementations.]{Latency of the windowing
    stage for no filter, Hann, and Hamming implementations processing 7\,670\,000 samples,
    averaged over 50 runs.}
    \label{fig:latency}
\end{figure}

All implementations take between 9 and 12\,ms, with no statistically significant
difference between the windowed and unwindowed cases. This indicates that the observed
latency is dominated by data handling overhead rather than the windowing computation
itself, and confirms that window selection can be made independently of hardware
configuration.

The experiments were then extended to the Blackman, Kaiser, and Taylor windows. Using dataset DS2,
the multi-receiver signal of a target sweep azimuth, elevation and range, fifteen filter
configurations were applied to the data: \verb|Hann|, \verb|Hamming|, \verb|Blackman|,
\verb|Kaiser6|, \verb|Kaiser8|, \verb|Kaiser10|, \verb|Kaiser12|, \verb|Taylor1_30|,
\verb|Taylor2_30|, \verb|Taylor_3_30|, \verb|Taylor_4_30|, \verb|Taylor1_50|, \verb|Taylor2_50|
\verb|Taylor_3_50|, \verb|Taylor_4_50|. The parameter suffix of the Kaiser windows indicate the
\(\beta\) parameter and the two suffixes of the Taylor windows indicate \(\bar{n}\) and the
sidelobe level in -dB respectively.

Six noise intensities were applied to the data: no noise, and SNRs of -20, -10,
0, 10 and 20\,dB. Detections at ranges where the target is not present were labelled as
clutter, tallied, and their total power recorded. The results are shown in Figures
\ref{fig:recall_vs_clutter} and \ref{fig:recall_vs_clutter_power}.

\begin{figure}
    \incfig[0.8]{windowing_recall_vs_clutter}
    \caption[Recall vs clutter for different window functions.]{Recall vs number of clutter
    detections per ground truth for fifteen window configurations across six noise
    intensities.}
    \label{fig:recall_vs_clutter}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}
    \incfig[0.8]{windowing_recall_vs_clutter_power}
    \caption[Recall vs clutter power for different window functions.]{Recall vs total clutter
    power for fifteen window configurations across six noise intensities.}
    \label{fig:recall_vs_clutter_power}
\end{figure}

Recall decreases from 1 to 0.45 as noise increases, while clutter detections rise from 3
to 12 per ground truth, illustrating the fundamental trade-off between sensitivity and
clutter suppression. The Taylor windows calibrated to $-50$\,dB sidelobe levels achieve
the highest recall in the three noisiest conditions without introducing proportionally
more clutter than other windows, because their sidelobes are suppressed to the level at
which CFAR masks them. In terms of clutter power (Figure \ref{fig:recall_vs_clutter_power}),
the $-50$\,dB Taylor windows produce slightly higher clutter power than alternatives but
maintain superior recall under the highest noise intensities.

\section{Integration}
The integration stage is validated on synthetic test data from software scripts and the
test-source mode of the AWR2243 radar chips. Figure \ref{fig:integration-waterfall} shows
the expected SNR improvement with increasing integration window size on synthetic data.
Figure \ref{fig:integration-waterfall-orac} shows the output on AWR2243 test-source data,
which generates no noise and therefore produces no change in amplitude. The unchanged
amplitude confirms that the integration stage behaves correctly on data from the radar chips.
The subsystem can process data at a rate of 9.8 times the incoming data rate on the desktop
setup, and 3.8 times on the Xavier setup. The latency of the integration stage increases
with the integration's factor of reduction, since each thread performs all sum operations
for its corresponding range bin.

\begin{figure}
    \incfig[0.57]{integration_waterfall}
    \caption[Integration on synthetic data showing SNR improvement.]{Integration stage
    output on synthetic data. Top: low-SNR input waterfall with range on the y-axis and
    chirps on the x-axis; colour indicates power with yellow being most intense. Bottom:
    integrated output showing reduced noise and a more prominent target signal.}
    \label{fig:integration-waterfall}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}
    \incfig[0.57]{integration_waterfall_orac}
    \caption[Integration on AWR2243 test-source data.]{Integration stage output on
    AWR2243 test-source data across 8 channels, compressing 128 chirps into one with
    no noise present.}
    \label{fig:integration-waterfall-orac}
\end{figure}

The latency of the integration stage increases with the reduction factor, as shown in
Figure \ref{fig:ablation-cumulative-latency}. Much of this latency is due to the overhead
of handling data and launching the application. To isolate the per-frame processing cost,
the time taken to process is measured as a function of the number of input frames, allowing
the startup time to be approximated from the y-intercept of a line of best fit. This is
done for coherent integration, non-coherent integration, and no integration as a baseline,
with the pipeline patched to process empty buffers and avoid measuring data I/O overhead.
The results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:integration-latency-per-frame}.

\begin{figure}
    \incfig[0.7]{integration_throughput}
    \caption[Integration stage latency per frame.]{Processing time as a function of the
    number of input frames for coherent integration, non-coherent integration, and no
    integration. The y-intercept approximates pipeline startup time; the slope is the
    per-frame processing cost.}
    \label{fig:integration-latency-per-frame}
\end{figure}

The startup time is approximately 195\,ms across all three configurations. Once running,
coherent and non-coherent integration each add only 0.02\,ms per frame over the
no-integration baseline, confirming that the computational cost of the summation itself
is negligible and that integration length can be increased without significant latency
penalty.

Dataset DS2 sweeps azimuth, elevation and range across six noise regimes: -20, -10, 0, 10,
and 20\,dB SNR and no noise. SNRs were tuned to detections at the furthest ranges; closer
detections have higher SNRs than those listed. Figure \ref{fig:integration-setup}
illustrates the ground truths and 64 detections per ground truth for one of the noisy
scenarios. The recall and position error results are shown in Figures
\ref{fig:integration-angle-recall-per-segment} and \ref{fig:integration-RMS-position-error}.

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{integration_setup.png}
    \caption[Integration experiment ground truths and detections.]{Ground truths and
    detections for a target sweeping azimuth, elevation, and range in a noisy scenario.
    Each line segment connects a ground truth to its detection. Purple crosses mark ground
    truths with no detection within 1 degree in azimuth and elevation. The triangle marks
    the radar position and heading.}
    \label{fig:integration-setup}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}
    \incfig[0.9]{integration_angle_recall_per_segment}
    \caption[Recall Integration Experiment.]{Recall (proportion of ground truths with at
    least one detection within 1 degree in azimuth and elevation) as a function of
    integration length for coherent and non-coherent integration across six noise
    intensities.}
    \label{fig:integration-angle-recall-per-segment}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}
    \incfig[0.9]{integration_RMS_position_error}
    \caption[Position Error Integration Experiment.]{RMS position error as a function of
    integration length for coherent and non-coherent integration across six noise
    intensities.}
    \label{fig:integration-RMS-position-error}
\end{figure}

As integration length increases, recall improves for both strategies across all noise
intensities, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:integration-angle-recall-per-segment}. Coherent
integration consistently outperforms non-coherent integration. Through coherent integration
of 64 chirps, recall in the worst noise regime increased from near 0 to 0.9. Integration
also directly improves position accuracy, as shown in Figure
\ref{fig:integration-RMS-position-error}. Coherent integration performs slightly better
than non-coherent when integration length exceeds 4 chirps.
