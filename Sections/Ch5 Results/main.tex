\chapter{Results}
\section{Overview}
This chapter presents the validation process and results of the processes outlined in the 
Methodology (Chapter 
\ref{sec:methodology}). The results are organised by operation. Each section
discusses the correctness and computational performance of an algorithm. Additionally implementation 
decisions for each of the stages are compared for both their speed and accuracy. The results are 
presented as plots, and are accompanied by a discussion of their implications for the design 
of the radar processing pipeline in the following chapter. 

Before the per stage performance is presented, a wholistic view of the pipeline's performance is
given, including the tracking performance of the pipeline as a whole, and the contribution of each
stage to the overall latency of the pipeline.  

The pipeline demonstrates full real-time multi-target tracking functionality on each of the three
hardware platforms: the desktop workstation, the NVIDIA Xavier 
AGX, and the NVIDIA Xavier NX. On the more performant Jetson computer, a latency of less than 50mc 
while processing 4 videos streams of 256 chirps, each with 767 samples, per frame at a rate of 10 
frames per second is achieved.

On top of this, the harness used for visualisation can also run online, 
allowing the user to see the correct 
behaviour of each of the stages of the pipeline in real time. Examples of these visualisations are
for each of these stages are depicted in Appendix Figures  \ref{fig:fft-visual}, 
\ref{fig:cfar-visual}, \ref{fig:cluster-visual}, \ref{fig:angle-visual}, \ref{fig:track-visual1}, 
and \ref{fig:track-visual2}. These figures have been included as a reference for how to visualise
and validate the intermediate stages of a radar processing pipeline. Understanding these 
visualisations is also important for interpreting 
the following figures in this section: \ref{fig:integration-waterfall}, 
\ref{fig:integration-waterfall-orac}, \ref{fig:integration-setup}, \ref{fig:cfar-0.001-waterfall},
\ref{fig:clustering-results}, \ref{fig:angle-2d-plot}, \ref{fig:angle-3d-plot-noise},
\ref{fig:tracking-scenarios}, \ref{fig:tracking-3d-results}.

\subsection{Pipeline Ablation Study}
An ablation study is performed on each of the devices 
to determine the contribution of
each stage to the overall latency of the pipeline and their
marginal effects on its throughput. Latency is measured as 
the time between the arrival of a chirp to the capture stage of
the processing subsystem, and the
generation of the output of the pipeline, whatever it may be. 
Throughput is measured as the number of frames processed per 
second after processing 1000 frames. Dataset DS2 simulates the 
receiver signals of targets across a wide variety of ranges in
high noise. The pipeline is run on the data. On each trial,
a stage is added. The results are depicted in Figures
\ref{fig:ablation-latency-breakdown}, and \ref{fig:ablation-throughput-segments}.

From the figures depicting the cumulative latency of 
each of the pipeline stages (Figure
\ref{fig:ablation-latency-breakdown}), it can be seen that the latency
of the pipeline is highly dependent on the degree to which the input 
chirps are summed together in the integration stage of the processing
subsystem. Notably, the integrate stage contributes the most time to 
the pipeline latency when it is doing the least work (no integration,
outputting 256 chirps for every 256 input chirps). Figure 
\ref{fig:ablation-latency-breakdown} illustrates that the processing
application is mainly invariant to the number of segments, while 
the detection and tracking subsystem's latency increases 
proportionally with it. As a result, the first stage is faster 
than the second for segment counts above 4 and vice versa for
segment counts below 4. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \incfig[1]{Ablation_Latency_Breakdown}
    \caption{Pipeline latency breakdown as stages are added for 
    different integration segment counts (number of output chirps for 
    every 256 inputs chirps). Split into the processing subsystem
    the detection and tracking subsystem, 
    and a combined view.}
    \label{fig:ablation-latency-breakdown}
\end{figure}


\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \incfig[0.8]{Ablation_Dat_Throughput_Segments}
    \caption{Pipeline Throughput per Frame as stages are added, for 
    different integration segment counts (number of output chirps for 
    every 256 inputs chirps). Obtained by finding average frames 
    per second after processing 1000 frames each with 256 input chirps.}
    \label{fig:ablation-throughput-segments}
\end{figure}

While latency is generally between 20 and 50 ms for segment counts 
less than 64, the throughput of the pipeline is greater than 
5000 frames a second, or 0.2 ms per frame. This 
two-orders-of-magnitude difference between latency and 
throughput is due to the fact that multiple frames are processed 
simultaneously by the pipeline. While memory is available, a 
thread will be spun up to process each incoming frame.

\subsection{Tracking Performance on Real-world Data}
The radar hardware was taken to an open air field test site in New South Wales 
where it and a target drone were flown in a variety of scenarios. Figure 
\ref{fig:real-world-tracking} depicts one such air to air scenario. 
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{air-to-air.png}
    \caption{The radar is flown into the air and is pointed at a target drone carrying
    a reflective marker 200m away.}
    \label{fig:real-world-tracking}
\end{figure}

The results of the tracking subsystem failed to produce any tracks of the ariel target, 
due to high electrical noise at the beginning of each pulse that masked the close field
where the target was. Fortunately, the radar detected high cross-section targets 
in the distance. This is illustrated in the visualisation of the angle sensing stage
in Figure \ref{fig:points-over-map}, where detections from the radar line up with
a silo and trees.    

\begin{figure}

    building 706 m away, and trees surrounding a building 410 meters away. }
    \label{fig:points-over-map}
\end{figure}
