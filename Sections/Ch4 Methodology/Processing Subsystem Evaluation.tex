\section{Processing Subsystem Evaluation}
\label{sec:processing_app}
The proposed processing subsystem is responsible for receiving the raw radar data from the AWR2243
radar chips, depicted in Figure \ref{fig:AWR2243},
and preparing the data for detection. An instance of the subsystem runs per incoming video stream,
before outputting the data to the aggregator subsystem, which is upstream of the detection and
tracking subsystem. The subsystem performs the following operations sequentially: formatting, windowing, FFT, and 
integration, (see Figure \ref{fig:preprocessing-dataflow}). Below are the implementation and 
experiment details for each of the operations.

\subsection{Windowing}
% Zaknoun compared rect with Blackman Harris
To reveal target range from a single radar pulse, an \gls{fft} is applied to the
de-chirped pulse. 
% The  assumes the finite signal repeats periodically, so when the signal does
% not start and end at the same value, the implicit periodicity introduces a discontinuity. This
% causes energy from a true frequency to spread into adjacent bins — a phenomenon known as spectral
% leakage.

When an \gls{fft} is performed on a finite signal, it is implicitly multiplied by a rectangular window.
A term-wise multiplication in time corresponds to a convolution in the frequency domain, and the
rectangular window's sinc-shaped frequency response has large sidelobes that spread energy from
each frequency bin into its neighbours. By applying a more suitable window function prior to the
FFT, such as one with less prominent sidelobes that those of the rectangular window, spectral leakage 
can be reduced, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:hann filter}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[width=0.9\linewidth]{Images/ffts1}
    \caption[FFT with and without a Hann window showing spectral leakage reduction.]{Results of an \gls{fft} performed on a signal with two frequencies and Gaussian noise, with
    and without a Hann window. Without the Hann window, the bins close to the peak frequency bins 40
    and 45 are significantly elevated due to spectral leakage from those peaks, which would cause
    nearby bins to be falsely detected as targets.}
    \label{fig:hann filter}
\end{figure}

As established in the literature review (\ref{ch:literature_review}), window
choice is almost entirely unexamined as a comparative design variable in
miniature UAV radar: prior work either fixes a single window or compares 
a window against a rectangular window. 

This thesis expands on the limited analysis by evaluating five window functions applied to 
samples prior to the \gls{fft}: Hann,
Hamming, Blackman, Kaiser, and Taylor. Each window is a point-wise multiplication applied to the input
chirp data prior to the FFT, shaping the spectral leakage characteristics of the resulting
range-time map. The choice of window governs a fundamental trade-off between mainlobe width
(range resolution) and sidelobe attenuation (clutter rejection). These properties directly
affect downstream detection performance: a wider mainlobe reduces the ability to resolve closely
spaced targets, while higher sidelobes raise the effective noise floor and increase false alarm
rates in CFAR processing. The windows evaluated here are compared for their ability to maximise
true positive detections and minimise clutter power. The time-domain shape and frequency response
of all five windows are compared in Figure \ref{fig:windows_frequency_response}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \incfig[0.90]{windows_frequency_response}
    \caption[Time-domain shape and frequency response of the five window functions.]{Time-domain
    shape and frequency response of the five evaluated window functions. The Hann window reaches a
    first sidelobe level of approximately $-31.5$\,dB and the Hamming window approximately
    $-43$\,dB, while the three-term Blackman window suppresses its first sidelobe to approximately
    $-58$\,dB. The parametric Kaiser window is shown at $\beta = 3.0$ (first sidelobe approximately
    $-70$\,dB) and the Taylor window at $\text{SLL} = -35$\,dB with $\bar{n} = 4$ near-equiripple
    sidelobes ($N = 64$ samples). Wider mainlobes accompany greater sidelobe suppression across
    the family.}
    \label{fig:windows_frequency_response}
\end{figure}

The Hann window is a raised-cosine taper that smoothly reduces the signal to zero at both ends
of the aperture. It provides moderate sidelobe suppression with a first sidelobe level of
approximately $-31.5$\,dB, at the cost of a mainlobe width roughly twice that of the rectangular
window. It has no tunable parameters. For a sequence of length $N$, the Hann window is defined as

\begin{equation}
    w(n) = \frac{1}{2}\left[1 - \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N-1}\right)\right], \quad 0 \le n \le N-1.
    \label{eq:hann}
\end{equation}

The Hamming window is also a raised-cosine taper, but with a non-zero pedestal chosen to place a
null at the first sidelobe. This reduces the first sidelobe level to approximately $-43$\,dB,
improving on Hann in the near-sidelobe region, though the far sidelobes decay more slowly. Like
the Hann window, it has no tunable parameters. The Hamming window is defined as

\begin{equation}
    w(n) = 0.54 - 0.46\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N-1}\right), \quad 0 \le n \le N-1.
    \label{eq:hamming}
\end{equation}

The Blackman window extends the raised-cosine family to three harmonic terms, producing a first
sidelobe level of approximately $-58$\,dB. The steeper sidelobe roll-off comes at the cost of a
wider mainlobe (approximately $2.35$ bins at $-3$\,dB), making it better suited to environments
with strong clutter at the expense of reduced Doppler resolution. The standard Blackman window has
no tunable parameters and is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    w(n) = 0.42 - 0.50\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{N-1}\right)
               + 0.08\cos\!\left(\frac{4\pi n}{N-1}\right), \quad 0 \le n \le N-1.
    \label{eq:blackman}
\end{equation}

The Kaiser window is a parametric taper whose shape is controlled by a single
parameter $\beta$. Increasing $\beta$ raises sidelobe suppression at the cost of
a wider mainlobe, while $\beta = 0$ recovers the rectangular window. This
continuous tunability makes the Kaiser window well suited to applications where
the sidelobe level must meet a specific threshold, as $\beta$ can be chosen
analytically for a target attenuation without redesigning the window from scratch.
For a sequence of length $N$ the Kaiser window is defined as

\begin{equation}
    w(n) = \frac{I_0\!\left(\beta\sqrt{1 - \left(\dfrac{2n}{N-1} - 1\right)^{\!2}}\right)}
                {I_0(\beta)},
    \quad 0 \le n \le N-1,
    \label{eq:kaiser}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $I_0(\cdot)$ is the zeroth-order modified Bessel function of the
first kind, and representative values are $\beta \approx 5.0$ for sidelobe
attenuation near $-57$\,dB and $\beta \approx 8.6$ for approximately $-90$\,dB,
with mainlobe width increasing accordingly.

The Taylor window is a parametric taper designed to balance mainlobe width against
sidelobe suppression. Rather than enforcing a uniform sidelobe level across all
frequencies, it holds the first $\bar{n}$ sidelobes at or below a specified peak
sidelobe level (SLL) and allows the remaining sidelobes to decay monotonically
with increasing frequency offset. This mixed strategy produces a narrower mainlobe
than a purely equiripple design at the same SLL, at the cost of non-uniform far
sidelobes.

The two tunable parameters are the target peak sidelobe level $\text{SLL}$ (in dB,
typically $-25$\,dB to $-60$\,dB) and the number of nearly-equiripple sidelobes
$\bar{n}$ (commonly 4 or 5). Larger $|\text{SLL}|$ and larger $\bar{n}$ both
widen the mainlobe. The window coefficients are computed from a closed-form
expansion; for a sequence of length $N$ the Taylor window is

\begin{equation}
    w(n) = 1 + 2\sum_{m=1}^{\bar{n}-1} F_m \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi m}{N}\!\left(n - \frac{N-1}{2}\right)\right),
    \quad 0 \le n \le N-1,
    \label{eq:taylor}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the coefficients $F_m$ are derived from the design parameters $\text{SLL}$ and
$\bar{n}$. In this work the Taylor window is
evaluated at $\text{SLL} = -30, \text{and } -50$\,dB and $\bar{n} = 1,2,3, 
\text{and } 4$.

The metrics of clutter power, and sensitivity were identified as the most relevant for evaluating 
window performance. These values are obtained from the detector's response downstream; false detections
count towards clutter power and detections that match the range of a target are considered true positives.
The dataset on which this analysis is done is a sweep of azimuth elevation and range in a sweep of noise 
amplitudes using the pseudo-real dataset D4. The best window will be that which reduces clutter power while 
maintaining 
high sensitivity to targets, since both false alarms and missed detections are detrimental to 
\gls{daa} systems. The windows are also compared for their contributions to pipeline latency.

\subsection{Integration}
\label{sec:method-integration}
While existing literature has established the relative detection performance of
coherent and non-coherent integration for point-like targets
\cite{Richards2010}, the experiments in this thesis are instead centred on the
downstream computational effects of integration: the relationship between
integration parameters and hardware, and the trade-off between information loss,
sensitivity, and memory utilisation. The theoretical gains derived in
\S\ref{sec:background-radar-processing} do not determine which integration method 
is most suitable in a context where coherence is not guaranteed, and where 
compute resources are limited.

In this work, coherent integration is compared with non-coherent integration for their 
resulting SNR 
improvement, angle estimation quality, and track quality on dataset DS2. 
The relationship between coherent integration window size and the performance metrics 
is also explored.

the other stages and the integration window size. The trade-off between SNR and latency is then analysed. The integration stage is also evaluated in the
ablation study of the processing application.
