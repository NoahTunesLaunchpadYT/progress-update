\section{Miniature Compact Airborne Radar for UAV DAA}
The ASTM F3442/F3442M-23 standard outlines practical requirements for 
autonomous \gls{daa} systems. While the safe range from other 
aircraft is dependent on its type, speed, and whether it is cooperative 
with an \gls{adsb} system, the minimum range required is in no case 
less than 2,000 feet (609.6 m) horizontally and 250 feet (76.2 m) 
vertically \cite{astm_f3442_2023}. This forms a cylindrical volume 
referred to as the ``well clear zone''. On top of this, a DAA system 
should detect targets well in advance. Using the F3442 35 second 
lead time as a guide, a radar would need to have a range of 
6120 metres in order to detect and avoid fast travelling targets 
\cite{Wang2016}. 
Methodologies for 
producing detections on this scale 
each have advantages and limitations \cite{Yasin2020}. 

Radar enables long-range detections of small 
targets in all weather \cite{9635901} and provides direct range and velocity 
measurements \cite{skolnik2001radar}, which are critical for collision avoidance.
While radar has been previously too heavy and power-hungry for small UAVs 
\cite{Lies2020, McLain2015, Wang2016}, papers from as early as 2006 and 
2009 outline 
radar-based solutions with awareness of their miniaturisation potential 
\cite{4148337, 5438372}. 
Literature including works by Moses et al., Spencer, and Sysak et al., 
demonstrate this feasibility, but important problems remain before radar 
\gls{daa} can be widely adopted. 

Some of the first radar systems tested on small (<25 kg) \gls{uav}s were
shown in a set of feasibility studies by Moses, Rutherford, and Valavanis 
published in 2011 \cite{6044363, uavborne_moses_2011} that validate a 
miniature X-band radar system. One of the studies
\cite{6044363} mounts the 230 gram radar on a small quadcopter (see 
Figure \ref{fig:moses-quadcopter}), and successfully demonstrates target
detection and identification in a laboratory environment. Their 
evaluation emphasises target identification via micro-Doppler signatures rather
than ranging or tracking, achieving a 100\% detection rate when around three metres 
from the target. When distance increased and both platform and target took to the
air, accuracy dropped significantly --- a result that highlights the importance
of validating radar pipelines under realistic encounter conditions. The other
study of theirs tackles post-detection avoidance manoeuvres from a control perspective, 
but they do not conduct airborne tests to validate the system 
in realistic conditions. While the works together outline the software pipeline 
from end to end, they do not justify their choice in algorithms through
comparison with alternatives. They also do not 
characterise individual stage performances in areas such as latency or 
throughput. The present thesis work compares the pipeline stages chosen by 
Moses et al. such as a Hamming window and a peak detector,
along with alternatives, and measures their computation and tracking performances.   

A follow-up 2014 publication by this group (Moses et al.\ \cite{Moses2014})
presents an even smaller radar (see Figure \ref{fig:moses-2014}), now with 
angle sensing and a collision avoidance algorithm. The system is
evaluated indoors against large reflective targets. While the collision
avoidance algorithms are compared thoroughly, the algorithm for turning detections
into trajectories is not de, no other algorithm choices are
evaluated and readiness for open-air tests was not demonstrated --- the authors
themselves note that higher transmit power would be needed to detect targets 
at the ranges for safe UAV operation. The present thesis work aims to fill in the 
gap of 
no open-air trials through its own field demonstrations.

Shi et al. \cite{6712628}, Itcia et al. \cite{fmcw_itcia_2013} 
and Fasano et al. \cite{Giancarmine2016} each demonstrate airborne 
radar tracking on group 3 (greater than 25 kg) UAV platforms and of group 2 
(greater than 10 kg \cite{DoD2013UASRoadmap}) UAV targets at the kilometre 
ranges that Moses et al. 
didn't test, but on platforms too large to inform the small-UAV case. This 
thesis extends the work of these works to UAVs less than 25 kg in mass. 
\cite{Wang2016} builds upon Moses et al.'s work through the 
design and simulation of comparable \gls{swap} with  
ranging capabilities similar to those of Shi et al. and Itcia et al. 
The work begins to treat compute as resource to be allocated across
pipeline stages. They propose a low-SWaP \gls{lfmcw} architecture 
with six enabling techniques, two of which are compute allocation 
decisions: GPU accelerated FFT, and a floodlight-illumination-then-beamform
pipeline, avoiding the computationally expensive beamforming step until 
after detection. Simulated coverage varies from 4.4 km to 9.4 km depending 
on transmit power, beam-forming and FFT length, illustrating the impact of compute
allocation on detection range. Despite this, the allocation of compute is 
not measured. This thesis builds on Wang's work through measuring the allocation 
of compute between pipeline stages through an ablation study.

Spencer directly builds on the works of Moses et al.\, Shi et al.\, Itcia et al.\, 
and Fasano et al.\ by designing a compact four-element 250 gram phased array radar 
system that meets the SWaP constraints of a \gls{uav} less than 25 kg in mass 
\cite{compact_spencer_2015} (see Figure \ref{fig:spencer}).
Experiments reveal that the radar can detect targets of less than 0.1 m$^2$ 
cross-section at ranges of 150 metres. This makes the work one of the first
to demonstrate feasibility on this scale. The work found heavy 
clutter caused by electromagnetic interference, and demonstrated 
the use of the Levinson--Durbin algorithm to generate filter coefficients
\cite{levinson1947wiener, durbin1960fitting} 
to suppress static clutter, and the Recursive Random Sample
Consensus Algorithm (R-RANSAC) to maintain an accurate 
world model in the presence of clutter. The system block 
diagram is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:spencer dsp}, and 
resembles the pipeline of this thesis. Spencer's choice to 
perform CFAR after angle sensing directly contends with the design 
of Wang et al., who perform CFAR before angle sensing to save on compute. 
Spencer claims that per-channel CA-CFAR before angle sensing reduces 
sensitivity by the root of the number of channels. Whether a 
detection scheme exists that 
recovers multi-channel SNR gain without the cost of Spencer's per-cell 
angle sensing 
or whether it holds up under non-Gaussian clutter are open questions that this 
thesis addresses.

Lies et al.\ \cite{Lies2020, 9380893}, across two studies in 2020 and 2021, approach 
low-\gls{swap} \gls{daa} by treating algorithm 
implementation as a design variable that utilises a limited compute budget, 
a strategy adopted by this present thesis.  
The central architectural argument of the first work is a compute-allocation 
decision. By 
delegating angle sensing to a camera, the compute budget freed from blind 2D 
scanning is reallocated to SNR-recovering techniques — long dwell intervals, 
coherent integration, and correction of phase drift and range bin migration — that 
extend the range of a low-cost 77~GHz chipset from ~120~m (measured against a 
hovering DJI Mavic~2) to a link-budget-extrapolated ~1~km against a \gls{vtol}-sized 
target. Notably, instead of demonstrating one solution to the radar detection problem, 
the 2021 study compares four range-
estimation algorithms (FFT, discrete polynomial transform, noncoherent integration, 
spectral entropy) against a success-probability metric, and derives an explicit 
compute-vs-fidelity tradeoff for its motion model. It is also 
one of the few works to measure phase drift and range bin migration on a real 
target at long dwell, documenting the platform-motion 
artefacts that other works do not characterise. Three gaps remain: the comparison 
is confined to a single pipeline 
stage; validation is in simulation under white Gaussian noise, with the 
experimental campaign restricted to ground-based measurement of a hovering target; 
and the reallocated compute budget itself is quantified in Big-O rather than 
measured in latency or throughput on a specific platform. This thesis extends Lies'
s methodology to the full detection, angle-estimation, and tracking pipeline, 
measures the compute allocation empirically across embedded hardware platforms, 
and validates under the non-Gaussian noise regime that arises in genuinely 
airborne encounter conditions.

Sysak et al.\
\cite{sysak2025_lightweight_compact_pulse_radar_uav_collision_avoidance} (2025)
present the most recent and complete system-level demonstration in the surveyed 
literature: a lightweight X-band pulse radar for mid-air collision avoidance on
medium UAV platforms, using a fixed forward-looking antenna and FPGA-based pulse
compression. In a ground-based field campaign, the radar reliably detected large
static urban structures at 1--3\,km, though
detection is characterised almost exclusively against large, high-\gls{rcs}
targets under low, spatially uniform clutter using a single CA-CFAR detector,
leaving the sensitivity floor against small moving airborne targets
uncharacterised. A separate trial detected a 3.15\,m-wingspan UAV at 6.4\,km
(see Figure~\ref{fig:sysak}), demonstrating kilometre-scale detection within a
medium UAV power budget, but without systematic sensitivity statistics. The 
validated system performs range
estimation only, deferring angle sensing to a future upgrade, and although a
range-rate tracker and \gls{tcpa} logic are described, no tracking-quality or
latency results are reported. Sensitivity against small moving airborne targets, 
pipeline completeness through angle sensing and tracking, and the latency of the 
resulting configuration are all questions this present thesis investigates.

Taken together, these works establish that radar hardware small and low-power 
enough for \gls{uav} \gls{daa} is feasible. Two questions about the software 
pipelines that run on it remain open. First, a tight compute budget has been 
consistently recognised but never measured across the surveyed decade of miniature 
\gls{uav} \gls{daa} research. Second, Wang and Spencer disagree on the optimal 
ordering of CFAR and angle sensing, and no subsequent work has resolved the 
question empirically. This thesis takes up both.

\section{GPU-based Radar Processing Under SWaP Constraints}

Historically, literature that explores the software pipelines for radar 
processing on embedded platforms have focused on \gls{fpga}s to achieve
high performance with low power and weight. Shi et al.\ \cite{6712628}, (whose
airborne platform is discussed above), 
describe a small \gls{fmcw} phased array built from off-the-shelf development 
boards, validating that continuous real-time 2D FFTs are achievable on a 100 
MHz FPGA. However, processing was split between an onboard FPGA and offboard 
post-processing, and signal coherence problems prevented real-world validation. % kinda related to the problem that Lies solves
Newmeyer et al.\ \cite{7577322} go further, demonstrating a 120 gram, 8 Watt 
\gls{fmcw}
phased array pipeline on a Zynq ARM System-on-Chip. Through closely monitoring 
power, latency
distributions, and hardware resources the work establishes that embedded 
compute platforms can run radar processing algorithms in real time, even 
under strict SWaP constraints. However, only one hardware
configuration and one algorithm sequence are evaluated. 

The efficiency of FPGAs comes at the cost of long development cycles, and 
hardware description expertise. The maturation of high level parallel 
programming frameworks such as \gls{cuda} and the arrival of small but 
powerful embedded GPU modules such as NVIDIA's Jetson line, has made 
\gls{gpu}s a practical alternative to \gls{fpga}s for 
embedded radar processing. This is reflected by a growing body of work 
that demonstrates the feasibility of GPU acceleration for radar processing. 
For these reasons, this thesis focuses on GPU acceleration of radar 
processing pipelines. 

Zhao et al.\ \cite{rs15225421} implement CUDA acceleration for a passive
bistatic radar pipeline spanning clutter suppression, range--Doppler
processing, and CA-CFAR detection, reporting a maximum acceleration ratio of
113.13$\times$ over a CPU baseline, but omit angle estimation, coordinate
transforms, and tracking, and do not discuss memory reallocation or thread
synchronisation costs. Fang et al.\
\cite{Fang2024} cover more of the chain, including \gls{mti}, \gls{mtd}, clutter map
detection, point fusion, and trajectory tracking. The design achieves an 11$\times$
speedup over a CPU implementation without loss of track quality (see Figure
\ref{fig:fang_pipeline}), but only evaluates a single platform and pipeline configuration. 
Mondal et al.\ \cite{Mondal2024_gpu_radar} provide the
closest treatment of implementation detail, analysing memory-access and
parallelism optimisations per stage across \gls{mti}, Pulse Doppler Processing,
and CA-CFAR, but their evaluation is confined to a single desktop-class NVIDIA
Quadro FX~1800, architecturally distinct from the Jetson-class edge platforms
typical of autonomous drones, and to a single pipeline configuration. Zaknoun
\cite{zaknoun2024_realtime_gpu_accelerated_radar_processing} treats GPU radar
software architecture for real-time processing in depth, but outside a UAV DAA
context and without evaluation under UAV SWaP constraints. The pipeline lands
packets of raw radar data directly onto the GPU, bypassing CPU staging and 
host-device transfer, and finds a bottleneck in CPU-side post-processing.

Read together, these works establish that embedded GPUs can accelerate the
radar chain by an order of magnitude, but they divide on what counts as a
meaningful performance claim. Zhao, Fang, and Mondal each benchmark against a
CPU baseline, a comparison Zaknoun calls close to trivial, since GPU dominance 
over CPU on massively parallel workloads is already well established. He argues 
the more informative measure is the fraction of a GPU's peak throughput an 
implementation actually attains.
Similar metrics are not absent from the wider literature: Newmeyer et al.\
\cite{7577322}, working under SWaP constraints where energy-per-operation is
the natural currency, report throughput and energy-per-FFT rather than a
headline ratio. The open question is therefore not whether GPUs help, but how
much of their potential a given implementation reaches, a quantity that shifts
with hardware, parallelisation strategy, and memory layout, and that no
single-configuration speedup ratio can reveal. This motivates the present
thesis's treatment of low-level implementation as a set of design variables
that can change between hardware contexts.

\section{Detection of Small Targets in Low-SNR Regimes}

The detection and angle-estimation stages of the pipeline are the ones
most exposed to the low-\gls{snr}, cluttered conditions that a small
target at kilometre range produces. Two strategies for coping with this
regime recur in the literature: recovering sensitivity by treating
detection and angle estimation jointly rather than sequentially, and
abandoning the Gaussian clutter model that classical detectors assume.

Aubry et al.\
\cite{aubry2020_single_pulse_simultaneous_detection_angle_estimation_multichannel}
challenge the convention, which they trace through the monopulse
literature, that angle estimation is a separate stage activated only
after a detection event is triggered. Instead they detect and estimate
angle jointly from a single pulse across a multichannel array, which
recovers sensitivity to targets not visible in any individual channel
and avoids the brittleness of thresholding each channel independently
under low SNR.
The result is established, however, only for single simulated targets
in homogeneous Gaussian interference. The limitations of this work
motivate the extension of the detection and angle-sensing algorithm to
non-Gaussian interference, multiple targets, and stationary clutter.
% make sure to tie this thread to the Wang Spencer debate from section 1 in section 4.
% alluding to the work of spencer

An et al.\
\cite{an2024_time_varying_angle_estimation_unresolved_extended_targets_monopulse}
propose an efficient method for resolving multiple unresolved targets
within a single monopulse cell, but evaluate it only under simulated
white-noise conditions without integration into an end-to-end pipeline.
Explicit failure modes exist for same-angle targets and cells containing
three or more targets. The work motivates an investigation into these
failure modes of monopulse angle sensing and mitigation strategies.

Ezuma et al.\ \cite{ezuma2019microuav} demonstrate that the Gaussian and
Rayleigh clutter assumptions that both of the above works rely on fail
for ground-based mmWave radar detecting UAVs. They find that a Weibull
distribution provides a significantly better fit to measured clutter
statistics, and explicitly note that prior UAV detection work had
neglected this. This finding directly undermines the Gaussian noise
assumption of the CFAR algorithm and the noise assumptions of many of
the simulation-based studies. This leaves the selection of an appropriate
detection algorithm an open question that is investigated in this work.
The airborne case introduces further departures from the homogeneous
Gaussian noise model: a vibrating platform subject to altitude variation,
aspect angle changes, and platform-induced interference all corrupt the
assumptions underpinning standard windowing and CFAR algorithm selection.
No published work has measured or modelled these statistics for a
miniature airborne \gls{fmcw} radar in real flight conditions.

Taken together, the detection and angle-estimation techniques best suited
to small, low-SNR targets have been validated only under idealised noise,
and none has been characterised inside a complete pipeline or measured for
latency on representative hardware. Which detector and angle-estimation
choices survive genuinely airborne, non-Gaussian clutter, and at what cost,
are therefore questions this thesis resolves empirically.

\section{Summary and Research Gap}

Across the three areas surveyed above, the literature makes meaningful
progress in three directions. Compact radar hardware has been demonstrated on
platforms as small as a quadcopter \cite{6044363, Moses2014}, with some works
achieving detection ranges beyond 5 km \cite{Wang2016}. GPU and embedded
compute platforms have been shown capable of accelerating the core stages of a
radar signal processing chain by an order of magnitude or more
\cite{Fang2024, rs15225421, Mondal2024_gpu_radar}. And angle estimation
algorithms capable of resolving closely-spaced or low-SNR targets have been
proposed \cite{an2024_time_varying_angle_estimation_unresolved_extended_targets_monopulse,
aubry2020_single_pulse_simultaneous_detection_angle_estimation_multichannel}.

However, the field consistently falls short in three areas. First, no work
validates a complete sensing-to-tracking pipeline under realistic airborne
encounter conditions against small moving targets. Hardware demonstrations
use ground-based setups, large reflective targets, or post-processed
data \cite{sysak2025_lightweight_compact_pulse_radar_uav_collision_avoidance,
6044363, Moses2014}. Simulation-only studies make no attempt at empirical
validation \cite{Wang2016, Lies2020}. As a result, the relative
merit of competing algorithm choices for CFAR, angle estimation, and tracking
has not been assessed under the (non-Gaussian \cite{ezuma2019microuav}) 
noise conditions and target characteristics
representative of small UAV encounters. Windowing in particular is used but 
rarely examined: of the
surveyed works, only three employ a sidelobe-suppressing window at all, and only
Zaknoun \cite{zaknoun2024_realtime_gpu_accelerated_radar_processing} compares
alternatives, contrasting a rectangular window against Blackman--Harris. The
mainlobe--sidelobe trade-off and its downstream effect on detection therefore
remain uncharacterised for this application. Further down the pipeline, there exists 
a disagreement between Wang et al. and Spencer over whether CFAR should precede 
or follow angle sensing \cite{Wang2016,compact_spencer_2015} and An et al.'s 
monopulse algorithm failure modes are uncharacterised under realistic noise 
conditions.

Second, GPU-acceleration studies report large aggregate speedups, but a headline 
ratio against a CPU baseline reveals little about how much of a platform's 
throughput an implementation actually reaches. Nor do these studies treat the 
implementation of an algorithm as a design variable: no work systematically 
compares implementations with different parallelisation strategies, memory layouts
, or host--device transfer patterns, or measures the per-stage latency these 
choices produce. This is distinct from the choice of algorithm and its ordering in 
the first gap: the same algorithm admits many implementations, and their cost has 
not been measured on representative hardware.

Third, existing GPU studies evaluate a single hardware platform, typically a 
desktop GPU \cite{rs15225421, Mondal2024_gpu_radar} or a non-UAV embedded system 
\cite{zaknoun2024_realtime_gpu_accelerated_radar_processing}. Several of the works 
surveyed acknowledge that algorithm choice is driven by available compute: Spencer 
applies an arbitrary threshold before angle sensing to reduce load 
\cite{compact_spencer_2015}, and Lies et al.\ delegate angle sensing to a camera 
to free 
budget for SNR recovery \cite{Lies2020}. Yet no work systematically examines how 
hardware selection changes the range of viable algorithms and implementations. 
Given that embedded GPU architectures differ substantially from desktop GPUs in 
memory bandwidth, parallelism, and thermal constraints, conclusions drawn from one 
platform cannot be assumed to generalise.

This thesis addresses these gaps by characterising detection and tracking
sensitivity against small moving airborne targets across a representative
selection of algorithm choices, by systematically comparing low-level
implementations of those algorithms and quantifying their effects on
end-to-end throughput and latency, and by evaluating how both sets of
conclusions vary across embedded hardware configurations to produce
hardware-aware design recommendations.
