\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature_review}

\section{Compact Airborne Radar for UAV DAA}

The work of Sysak et al.\
\cite{sysak2025_lightweight_compact_pulse_radar_uav_collision_avoidance},
Wang et al.\ \cite{Wang2016}, and Moses et al.\ \cite{6044363, Moses2015}
each tackle important subproblems in the design of a radar DAA system for
small UAVs, but each leave important gaps unaddressed in the literature.

Sysak et al.\
\cite{sysak2025_lightweight_compact_pulse_radar_uav_collision_avoidance}
present a lightweight X-band monopulse radar for mid-air collision avoidance
on medium UAV platforms, integrating a multi-channel forward-looking antenna
with FPGA-based pulse compression on a single 60 gram PCB, with average power
below 80 Watts. Field trials demonstrate reliable detection of large obstacles
at 1--3 km (see Figure \ref{fig:sysak}), as well as detection of a 3.15 m
wingspan UAV from 6.4 km. The work validates a simple online pipeline from
range compression to \gls{tcpa} estimation, but does not characterise the
pipeline's latency or tracking performance, nor explain whether detections
were made during live sensing or post-processed from recorded data.

    \incfig[0.9]{sysak_radar}
    \caption{Sysak et al.'s experimental set up and results. The radar is
    capable of detecting large targets at 1--3 km with high ranging accuracy.
    We observe peaks in amplitude over range that correspond to target position.}
    \label{fig:sysak}
\end{figure}

Moses et al.\ \cite{6044363} introduce a 230 gram miniature radar mountable
on a small quadcopter (see Figure \ref{fig:moses-quadcopter}), demonstrating
the feasibility of integrating radar sensing onto very small platforms. Their
evaluation emphasises target identification via micro-Doppler signatures rather
than ranging or tracking, achieving a 100\% detection rate at 7 metres from
the target. When distance increased and both platform and target took to the
air, accuracy dropped significantly --- a result that highlights the importance
of validating radar pipelines under realistic encounter conditions. The work
provides a dataflow diagram for the processing pipeline but does not
characterise stage latency, compare alternative algorithmic choices, or
report the final weight, size, and power consumption of the system.

A follow-up 2014 publication by the same group (Moses et al.\ \cite{Moses2015})
presents an even smaller radar (see Figure \ref{fig:moses-2014}) with angle
sensing capabilities and collision avoidance algorithms. Here the radar is
evaluated only indoors against large reflective targets. While the collision
avoidance algorithms are compared thoroughly, no other algorithm choices are
evaluated and readiness for open-air tests was not demonstrated --- the authors
themselves note that higher transmit power would be needed.

Wang et al.\ \cite{Wang2016} demonstrate a complete low-SWaP LFMCW system
design for group 2/3 UAS incorporating FFT-based detection, digital
beamforming, and GPU-accelerated \gls{fft} for update rate improvement. The
research establishes the feasibility of ranging targets beyond 5 km through
beamforming, but angle sensing is not explored, performance is validated in
simulation only, and compute and latency constraints are not considered.

Mohammad Karimi et al.\ \cite{Mohammadkarimi2024} support the feasibility of
electronically scanning to measure azimuth and elevation of targets, but
similarly validate ranging and angle estimation in simulation only. While the
work is mathematically rigorous in its treatment of \gls{daa} requirements,
empirical validation of the software pipeline under UAV SWaP constraints is
absent.

Taken together, these works establish the physical feasibility of miniature
radar for UAV DAA but consistently stop short of validating a complete
sensing-to-tracking pipeline under realistic airborne conditions.

\section{GPU-based Radar Processing Under SWaP Constraints}

Shi et al.\ \cite{6712628} describe a small \gls{fmcw} phased array built
from off-the-shelf development boards, validating that continuous real-time 2D
FFTs are achievable on a 100 MHz FPGA. However, processing was split between
an onboard FPGA and offboard post-processing, and signal coherence problems
prevented real-world validation.

Newmeyer et al.\ \cite{7577322} demonstrated a 120 gram, 8 Watt \gls{fmcw}
phased array pipeline on a Zynq ARM CPU, closely monitoring power, latency
distributions, and hardware resources --- clearly establishing that radar DAA
can operate under strict SWaP constraints. However, only one hardware
configuration and one algorithm sequence are evaluated.

Zhao et al.\ \cite{rs15225421} implement CUDA acceleration for a passive
bistatic radar pipeline, reporting up to a 113.13$\times$ speedup over a CPU
baseline across clutter suppression, range--Doppler processing, and CA-CFAR
detection. The study omits angle estimation, coordinate transforms, and Kalman
filtering, and does not discuss memory reallocation or thread synchronisation
costs.

Mondal et al.\ \cite{Mondal2024_gpu_radar} provide a per-stage analysis of an
entire radar processing pipeline on a GPU, describing techniques for optimising
memory access and parallelism across \gls{mti}, Pulse Doppler Processing, and
CA-CFAR. The evaluation was performed on a NVIDIA Quadro FX 1800, which
differs architecturally from the NVIDIA Jetson edge computers more commonly
used in autonomous drones, and only a single pipeline configuration is
assessed.

Fang et al.\ \cite{Fang2024} analyse a GPU-based pipeline performing
\gls{mti}, \gls{mtd}, clutter map detection, point fusion, and trajectory
tracking, achieving an 11$\times$ speedup over a CPU implementation without
loss of track quality (see Figure \ref{fig:fang_pipeline}).

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{fang2-p5-fang-large.png}
    \caption{Fang et al.'s GPU radar processing pipeline (source: \cite{Fang2024})}
    \label{fig:fang_pipeline}
\end{figure}

Zaknoun \cite{zaknoun2024_realtime_gpu_accelerated_radar_processing} provides
a comprehensive treatment of GPU radar software architecture for real-time
processing, though not situated in a UAV DAA context and without evaluation
under UAV SWaP constraints.

While these works demonstrate the potential of GPU acceleration for radar
processing, none benchmark a complete pipeline --- including angle estimation
and tracking --- on embedded GPU hardware representative of small UAV
platforms, nor do they profile host--device transfer and synchronisation
overhead explicitly.

\section{Sensitivity and Detection in Low-SNR Regimes}

An et al.\
\cite{an2024_time_varying_angle_estimation_unresolved_extended_targets_monopulse}
propose an efficient method for resolving two unresolved targets within a
single monopulse cell, but evaluate it only under simulated white-noise
conditions without integration into an end-to-end pipeline. Explicit failure
modes exist for same-angle targets and cells containing three or more targets.

Aubry et al.\
\cite{aubry2020_single_pulse_simultaneous_detection_angle_estimation_multichannel}
demonstrate that detection and angle estimation can be treated jointly in a
multichannel array, increasing sensitivity to targets not visible in any single
channel and avoiding brittleness of early thresholding under low SNR. Validation
is limited to single simulated targets in Gaussian interference.

Ezuma et al.\ \cite{ezuma2019microuav} demonstrate that Gaussian and Rayleigh
clutter assumptions --- the basis of CA-CFAR --- already fail for ground-based
mmWave radar detecting UAVs, finding that a Weibull distribution provides a
significantly better fit to measured clutter statistics, and explicitly noting
that prior UAV detection work had neglected this. The airborne case introduces
further departures from the homogeneous Gaussian noise model: a vibrating
platform subject to altitude variation, aspect angle changes, and
platform-induced interference all corrupt the assumptions underpinning standard
windowing and CFAR algorithm selection. No published work has measured or
modelled these statistics for a miniature airborne FMCW radar in real flight
conditions.

Existing angle estimation and detection algorithms have therefore been
validated only under idealised noise conditions that are unlikely to reflect
the environment experienced by a miniature airborne radar.

\section{Summary and Research Gap}

Across the three areas surveyed above, the literature makes meaningful
progress in three directions. Compact radar hardware has been demonstrated on
platforms as small as a quadcopter \cite{6044363, Moses2015}, with some works
achieving detection ranges beyond 5 km \cite{Wang2016}. GPU and embedded
compute platforms have been shown capable of accelerating the core stages of a
radar signal processing chain by an order of magnitude or more
\cite{Fang2024, rs15225421, Mondal2024_gpu_radar}. And angle estimation
algorithms capable of resolving closely-spaced or low-SNR targets have been
proposed \cite{an2024_time_varying_angle_estimation_unresolved_extended_targets_monopulse,
aubry2020_single_pulse_simultaneous_detection_angle_estimation_multichannel}.

However, the field consistently falls short in three areas. First, no work
validates a complete sensing-to-tracking pipeline under realistic airborne
encounter conditions against small moving targets. Hardware demonstrations
either use ground-based setups, large reflective targets, or post-processed
data \cite{sysak2025_lightweight_compact_pulse_radar_uav_collision_avoidance,
6044363, Moses2015}. Simulation-only studies make no attempt at empirical
validation \cite{Wang2016, Mohammadkarimi2024}. As a result, the relative
merit of competing algorithm choices for CFAR, angle estimation, and tracking
has not been assessed under the noise conditions and target characteristics
representative of small UAV encounters, leaving algorithm selection for this
regime without empirical grounding. Second, while GPU acceleration studies
demonstrate large aggregate speedups, they do not treat the implementation of
algorithms as a design variable. No work systematically compares
implementations with different parallelisation strategies, memory layouts, or
host--device transfer patterns. These are choices that directly determine the
latency, throughput, and memory requirements of the pipeline. Third, existing
GPU studies evaluate a single hardware platform, typically a desktop GPU
\cite{rs15225421, Mondal2024_gpu_radar} or a non-UAV embedded system
\cite{zaknoun2024_realtime_gpu_accelerated_radar_processing}. No work examines
how algorithm and implementation conclusions transfer across the embedded
hardware configurations available for UAV deployment. Given that embedded GPU
architectures differ substantially from desktop GPUs in memory bandwidth,
parallelism, and thermal constraints, conclusions drawn from one platform
cannot be assumed to generalise.

This thesis addresses these gaps by characterising detection and tracking
sensitivity against small moving airborne targets across a representative
selection of algorithm choices, by systematically comparing low-level
implementations of those algorithms and quantifying their effects on
end-to-end throughput and latency, and by evaluating how both sets of
conclusions vary across embedded hardware configurations to produce
hardware-aware design recommendations.
