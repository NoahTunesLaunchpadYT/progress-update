\chapter{Discussion}
In this chapter, we discuss the key findings within the results of the 
experiments with reference to the literature. Limitations 
are discussed through the chapter. After, recommendations are 
given. 

\section{Findings}
Understanding the relationship between the choice of windowing 
function and the downstream effect on detection, ranging and 
monopulse angle sensing is a major blind spot in the literature. 
While works such as Moses et al. \cite{6044363} and Shi et al.
\cite{6712628} find the Hamming window to be effective, and
Zaknoun et al.
\cite{zaknoun2024_realtime_gpu_accelerated_radar_processing}
find the Blackman-Harris window to be effective, the results
from this project indicate that the Taylor window with a
noise floor calibrated to the CFAR stage may be a
more appropriate alternative. By forcing the most prominent
sidelobes to the same low power level, CFAR is less likely to 
mistake them for targets. That being said, more work is required 
to support the claim that the Taylor window is more appropriate. 
It has not yet been validated in the noise regime of dataset DS3.
A further limitation is that the adverse effects of its wide main 
lobe were avoided by testing it only on single-target scenarios, so its tendency to 
blur closely spaced targets was never exercised.
Because a wider main lobe means lower resolution, 
the Taylor window may be less effective at resolving closely spaced
targets than windows with thinner main lobes such as the rectangular 
and Hamming windows. While more windows were explored 
in this thesis compared to other literature in the UAV DAA radar domain, 
five filters is still a small subset of possible window functions 
and future work should explore a wider variety of windows, such as
the Blackman-Harris, explored in 
\cite{zaknoun2024_realtime_gpu_accelerated_radar_processing}, with 
a variety of parametrisation. 

The insignificant time difference between each of the implementations of the windowing 
stage (including no implementation) is expected, given that across all implementations, 
the window is just a term-wise multiplication on the GPU. The ablation study revealed 
that the windowing function had minimal impact on the desktop and Jetson platforms, 
indicating that window selection can be made independently of hardware configuration. 
The pipeline validation trial on DS4, however, offers early evidence that window 
selection is not independent of the noise regime. The increase in false tracks 
suggests that synergy between the Taylor window and CFAR degrades under non-
Gaussian noise. This motivates a more comprehensive investigation of the 
failure modes of each of the filters in the noise regimes of miniature radar.

The results from the coherent integration experiment find
that the signal
to noise ratio improvement aligns with the expected "square root of N" relationship. 
The improvement creates a positive downstream effect on the
ability of CFAR to detect weak
targets. Concretely, coherent integration of 64 chirps raised detection recall
from near zero to 0.9 in the worst noise regime tested, demonstrating that
integration length is one of the most impactful single parameters for extending
detection range against weak targets. Non-coherent integration similarly improves the signal-to-noise
ratio, but to a lesser extent. In addition to improving sensitivity to targets,
the integration stage is found to be 
an effective lever at increasing throughput, as demonstrated by the pipeline
throughput experiment. Although large integration sizes require more sums, 
these operations are cheap on the GPU, and are overshadowed by the cost of 
outputting data, and the cost of later stages having more data to process.  
Still, the large amount of work that each thread has to 
do, summing over many chirps, motivates the exploration of more parallelised 
implementations that distribute the sums between threads. The major limitation
of the analysis of the integration stage so far has been in assuming that 
targets are stationary across the integrated chirps, assuming full
phase coherence, which is not the case for faster moving targets, 
that create changing Doppler shift and phase shift as radio-wave path
length changes. Future work should explore the effect of target velocity 
on the performance of the integration stage, and the downstream effect 
on CFAR and angle sensing performance.

The CFAR results outline a method for determining a threshold from a 
desired false alarm rate using the power sum from multiple receivers. 
The experiments demonstrate the GPU implementation
is most appropriate for the Xavier embedded platform, regardless of the size 
of the buffer for all practical window sizes, but on the desktop hardware, 
the prefix sum approach is more efficient until more than 50 chirp records need to be 
processed at a time. The difference between hardware
platforms highlights the importance of hardware-aware algorithm selection. The 
results of the angle sensing stage demonstrate the capability of the algorithm 
to detect multiple targets with different bearing/elevation ratios. The ablation 
study illustrates that CFAR dominates the latency 
of the tracking subsystem. The relatively high cost of CFAR motivates the 
exploration
of more efficient CFAR implementations. Potential approaches include implementing 
the prefix sum table approach on the GPU, using a reduction approach to calculate the 
noise floor, and more consciously minimising cache misses within warps. The works
by Mondal et al. \cite{Mondal2024_gpu_radar} and Zhao et al. \cite{rs15225421}
also draw attention to the importance of memory layout to maximise the 
cache efficiency of GPU implementation of CA-CFAR. This is an important
consideration for future work on optimising the CFAR stage. 

The open question of how to order angle sensing and CFAR is addressed
through the application of a technique that uses the power sum of multiple receivers 
to calculate the CFAR threshold instead of the power sum of a single receiver. 
The technique attains the full sensitivity that Spencer \cite{compact_spencer_2015}
sacrificed efficiency to achieve. The technique proved correct in simulation, but 
has not yet been validated on real-world data, which is the principal limitation of this contribution.

Comparing CA-CFAR, GO-CFAR, and SO-CFAR under different noise intensities
reveals that CA-CFAR is more effective at detecting targets in high
noise conditions while maintaining low clutter volume and power. This is 
likely due to CA-CFAR having a less volatile noise floor estimate, averages 
across
twice as many training cells as GO-CFAR and SO-CFAR which only average 
across half as many. One possible source of
bias in the results is that the GO-CFAR and SO-CFAR implementations' alpha 
values were taken from those of CA-CFAR, causing GO-CFAR to slightly 
over-mask and SO-CFAR to slightly under-mask. A more fair comparison would
involve tuning the alpha values of each implementation to achieve the same
false alarm rate, and then comparing the detection performance of each. 
A limitation of this comparison is that it was performed only on the synthetic datasets 
DS1 and DS2, which feature purely Gaussian noise, whereas the real-world dataset DS3 
features non-Gaussian noise; the ranking of the three detectors may not hold in that regime.
Future work should explore the 
interaction of the different CFAR implementations with the noise regime 
of DS3. Investigating other forms of detection algorithms, such as machine learning-based
detectors, moving target indication (MTI) filters, and ordered statistics
CFAR (OS-CFAR) (used in Newmeyer et al. \cite{7577322}), would provide 
a more comprehensive understanding of the design space for the detection 
stage of radar pipelines. 

A limitation of the clustering analysis is that it rests on visual inspection of the 
output rather than quantitative metrics, and that only one algorithm, contiguous block 
clustering, was implemented and evaluated. Future work should explore a
wider variety of clustering algorithms, such as DBSCAN, and evaluate them
using more quantitative metrics, such as true positive rate, false positive 
rate, and the downstream effect on track quality and angle sensing
accuracy. Clustering is an important stage for tracking multiple targets, 
and becomes increasingly important as targets become more closely spaced,
and when multiple receivers are detecting the same target. Future work
should explore the performance of different clustering algorithms under
these difficult conditions. Both this work and Newmeyer et al.'s 
\cite{7577322} found clustering to be an effective method for obtaining
sub-range bin resolution, but a gap in research still remains regarding 
clustering stage design for miniature radar. 

The monopulse angle sensing stage's results demonstrate the capability
of the algorithm to detect multiple targets with different bearing/elevation ratios,
and high accuracy in low and moderate noise conditions. In high noise,
repeated chirps and integration effectively increase the SNR. Although individual
detections become noisy and inaccurate, the angle estimates remain unbiased:
accumulating detections over time produces clusters centred on the true target
positions, confirming that the algorithm functions as an unbiased estimator of
bearing and elevation even at $-10$\,dB SNR when temporal integration is applied
upstream. The main limitation of the current 
design of the angle sensing stage is its susceptibility to targets
that are close in range, but have different bearing/elevation ratios. 
Because they occupy the same range bin, they are treated as a single target
with a bearing/elevation ratio that is the power-weighted average of the 
two targets, and there is no mechanism for identifying how many targets
exist in a given range bin. Monopulse target separation strategies 
presented in An et al. 
\cite{an2024_time_varying_angle_estimation_unresolved_extended_targets_monopulse}
is highly relevant to this problem, and future work should explore its application 
to real world data. Additionally, the joint detection and angle estimation approach 
of Aubry et al. 
\cite{aubry2020_single_pulse_simultaneous_detection_angle_estimation_multichannel}
was not explored in this project, but may be a promising approach to improving the 
angle sensing performance in multi-target scenarios. 

The final stage of the pipeline, the tracking stages, demonstrates a modular, 
tunable multi-target tracking approach that is effective at tracking targets in 
all tested noise regimes in scenarios where targets are separated in range. 
Mahalanobis distance-based gating is effective at associating detections to
tracks, the associator is sufficiently fast for real-time detection-to-track 
association, and the Kalman filter is effective at smoothing tracks, providing
predictions for gating, and applying measurement uncertainty to target state 
estimation. Track initiation, promotion and termination are effective at
managing a small number of targets even in high clutter. We found that 
a small amount of noise actually improves track quality, because 
spectral artifacts from windowing get washed out by noise and are less
likely to be misinterpreted as faint targets. Due to signal strength 
depending on the fourth power of range, the tracker had a hard time 
maintaining a track of the farthest target in -20 dB SNR conditions, 
with recall dropping from 67\% to 33\% when compared to the lower intensity 
noise scenarios. We also found that tracker position error estimates 
stay accurate (less than 22 m error RMS) in all noise regimes. This is
due to the smoothing that the Kalman filter provides. While the tracker is
effective at tracking targets in the DS2, and DS4 datasets, it has not yet been 
validated on the real-targets dataset DS3, which leaves questions about how
the tracker performs in real life. Only one tracking algorithm was implemented in 
this work, and future work should compare this proof of concept to a wider
variety of tracking algorithms, such as the multiple hypothesis tracker (MHT),
the joint probabilistic data association filter (JPDAF), and the probability 
hypothesis density (PHD) filter. Algorithms that better utilise detection 
power may be more effective in identifying and rejecting clutter. 
In addition to comparing different tracking algorithms, future work should 
also explore the effect of different tuning parameters, such as the gating
threshold, and the track promotion and termination thresholds.  

The field trials, in which the radar and a target drone were flown in air-to-air 
scenarios, exposed a limitation not visible in simulation. The tracking subsystem 
failed to produce any tracks of the aerial target, owing to strong coupling between the 
transmitter and receiver that saturated the beginning of every signal and masked the 
near-field region where the target was located. The radar did, however, detect high 
cross-section stationary targets at longer range, with detected points corresponding 
closely to a silo, a building, and a stand of trees at ranges of roughly 400 to 700 
metres. 
This outcome is the most significant limitation of the present work: while the pipeline 
is shown to track targets across all tested noise regimes in simulation, its real-world 
performance against small aerial targets remains unproven. The cause was a hardware-
level coupling artefact rather than a shortcoming of the processing algorithms, but the 
limitation on real-world validation stands regardless.

The trials were nonetheless valuable in two respects. They 
confirmed the detection and angle-sensing stages against real high cross-section returns
, and they provided a recording of genuine field noise, including a large DC offset 
that manifested as a zero-frequency peak and produced static detections at the minimum 
and maximum range. Addressing the transmitter-receiver coupling, whether through 
hardware isolation or a near-field blanking stage, is a prerequisite for validating the 
tracker against live aerial targets and is a priority for future work.

The field-noise recording did, however, enable a closer approximation to real-world 
conditions through the pseudo-real dataset DS4, which sums the recorded noise with a 
simulated target flying a snaking trajectory through azimuth, elevation, and range. At 0
dB SNR, the irregular field noise produced more erratic track estimates and initiated 
some false tracks than the equivalent purely-simulated scenario. These observations 
corroborate the findings of Ezuma et al. \cite{ezuma2019microuav},
who notes the non-Gaussian nature of radar at this scale. This motivates future work
to characterise the noise regime for more realistic simulation. 

Despite the occasional false tracks, the tracker maintained a coherent track 
of the target 
throughout the trial (Figure \ref{fig:snake}). This 
is a meaningful partial validation: it demonstrates that the tracker's gating and 
Kalman smoothing remain robust to the non-Gaussian, DC-offset-laden noise of a real 
recording, even though the target signal itself is still simulated. 
The limitation of this partial validation is that, while the noise regime is realistic, the target return itself is still simulated, so phenomena such as multipath propagation, fluctuating cross-section, phase shift, and micro-Doppler are absent from the test.

This work addresses each of the three research gaps identified in the
literature review, but only partially. The comparison of windowing 
functions, coherent vs non-coherent integration, and CFAR variants
create a partial picture of how algorithmic selection affects 
performance of radar-based trackers. The GPU pipeline benchmarking 
across desktop and Xavier platforms, covering CFAR, angle
sensing, windowing, and coherent integration, provides insight to the 
relationship between tunable parameters such as buffer size, CFAR width,
and integration length, and the latency and throughput of the entire 
pipeline. This directly addresses the second gap, providing the 
hardware-constrained profiling
that prior UAV DAA radar work lacked. Comparisons between the desktop 
computer, NVIDIA Xavier NX, and NVIDIA Xavier AGX illustrate the impact of
architectural differences such as shared memory, which caused throughput to be
higher on the Jetson Xavier devices than on the desktop computer, possibly due
to near instant data migration between the CPU and GPU. Additionally for the 
CFAR algorithm, the fastest implementation was different between 
hardware platforms. These findings build towards filling in the third  
literature gap, a lack of hardware-aware
design for low SWaP radar processing pipelines. 

\section{Design Recommendations}
The windowing results support the selection of the Taylor window for minimising 
clutter detections, and the Hamming window where resolution of closely spaced targets 
is a priority, given its narrow main lobe. The global spectral leakage penalty of
the Taylor and Hamming windows is acceptable in practice; noise does not need to be
minimised to zero, it only needs to be low enough to get masked out by CFAR.
As the latency contribution of the windowing stage is negligible and invariant to 
window choice, the selection can be made purely on detection performance grounds.

For coherent integration, the results support its use over non-coherent integration 
for slow or stationary targets, due to its superior SNR 
improvement. The marginal cost of the summation itself means that integration length can 
be increased without significant latency penalty. In fact, the more chirps 
integrated, the less data is being outputted, and the higher the throughput of the 
pipeline.

CA-CFAR is recommended over GO-CFAR and SO-CFAR for miniature radar operating in high 
noise conditions, given its more stable noise floor estimate from the larger training 
cell count. A GPU-based implementation is preferable on the Xavier embedded platform 
across all practical training cell window sizes. On the desktop, the prefix sum CPU 
implementation is competitive for small buffer sizes, but the GPU implementation is 
preferable at larger buffer sizes. The crossover point differs between platforms, 
confirming that implementation selection cannot be made platform-agnostically. As CFAR 
dominates the latency of the detection subsystem, with angle sensing contributing a 
factor of 1.5 to 5 times less, optimisation effort should be directed at the CFAR stage 
rather than the angle sensing stage. Priority approaches include the prefix sum table 
method, reduction-based noise floor estimation, and minimising cache misses within warps.

For the clustering stage, contiguous block clustering is a practical starting point for 
obtaining sub-range bin resolution, but its evaluation should be accompanied by 
quantitative metrics rather than visual inspection alone. The choice of clustering 
algorithm becomes increasingly consequential as targets become more closely spaced, and 
this should be a primary consideration when selecting an algorithm for a given operational scenario.

For monopulse angle sensing, the results support its use in low to moderate noise 
conditions with good accuracy. In high noise conditions, coherent integration should be 
applied upstream to improve SNR before angle estimation. The primary design limitation 
is the handling of multiple targets within the same range bin, which should be addressed 
through target separation strategies before deployment in dense traffic scenarios.

The overhead of host-device data transfer and thread synchronisation constitutes a 
substantial portion of remaining pipeline latency, and represents the primary target for 
further optimisation regardless of which stage implementations are selected.
